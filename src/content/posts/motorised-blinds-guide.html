The short answer: motorised blinds are worth it on windows that are hard to work by hand, such as tall glass, a row of windows side by side or a window out of reach, and when you want blinds to run on a schedule. On a small window you open and close every day, a manual chain usually works just as well and costs less. This guide explains how motorised (or motorized) blinds work, battery or wired, smart-home control, child safety, and how we decide where a motor actually makes sense.

How motorised blinds work

A motorised blind is the same blind with the chain replaced by a motor fitted at the top. Instead of pulling a chain, you raise and lower it with a remote, a wall switch, a phone app or a schedule, depending on the motor and how it is set up. Several blinds can be grouped so they move together.

At Kova, all three of our blind lines can be motorised for suitable sizes and setups: roller blinds, Venetian blinds and VertiSheer vertical sheer blinds.

Battery or wired?

  • Battery (no wiring): the motor runs on a rechargeable battery, so there is no cable to the window and no electrical work. It suits finished homes and rentals. You recharge it from time to time: the motor maker Somfy says once or twice a year is typical for its battery motors, depending on how big the blind is and how often it runs.
  • Hardwired: the motor is connected to your home's power, so there is no battery to recharge. It needs power at the top of the window, which is easiest to plan while you are still renovating, before ceilings and walls are finished.

If you are mid-renovation, tell us early. It is far simpler to plan wiring for a motor than to add it after the plaster and paint are done.

Smart-home control

We also offer HomeKit-compatible motors. With a compatible motor, Apple's Home app can close your blinds, include them in scenes and run automations, for example closing them when everyone leaves the house. This only works with a motor that supports HomeKit (or Matter), so if smart-home control matters to you, say so before we quote.

When a motor is worth it

  • Large or tall glass panels: full-height windows where a long chain becomes awkward and uneven.
  • Several blinds side by side: a row of windows that should open and close as one.
  • High or hard-to-reach windows: stairwells, mezzanines and windows above furniture.
  • Wide sliding doors: a VertiSheer across a big opening, where motorisation earns its place as the span grows.
  • Smart-home setups: when blinds should run on a schedule with the rest of the house.

When manual is the better choice

You don't need a motor on every window. For a small, everyday window or a smaller sliding door you use often, manual operation usually works perfectly well, and a manual system generally costs less than a motorised one. When we visit, tell us how you use each room and we will say honestly where a motor makes sense and where it doesn't, rather than adding it across the board.

Motorised blinds and child safety

Looped cords and chains on blinds are a known strangulation risk for babies and young children. The US Consumer Product Safety Commission says the safest option when young children are present is to go cordless, and the UK's Royal Society for the Prevention of Accidents (RoSPA) also advises choosing a cordless model or securing cords on a cleat at least 1.5 metres from the floor. A motorised blind has no operating cord or chain to hang down, which is one reason parents choose it for children's rooms. Whatever you choose, keep any remaining cords or chains tied up out of reach.

Looking after a motorised blind

  • Keep objects out of the blind's path while it runs.
  • Don't run the motor while you clean, and keep water away from the motor and its wiring (see our guide on how to clean blinds).
  • If a blind starts to behave oddly, stop it and call us rather than forcing it.
